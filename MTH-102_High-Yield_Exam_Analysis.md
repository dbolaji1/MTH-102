### Reasoning behind the ranking

**Why Limits is first.** It is the only topic that wins on *every single* criterion simultaneously. It has the highest term frequency in the repository (298), the largest combined page+slide footprint (~102), a third of the core textbook, a dedicated Conclusion *and* Summary, two dedicated weekly decks on top of its textbook session, the most glossary entries, and — decisively for exam preparation — **the largest bank of practice questions anywhere in the repository (43 numbered exercises)**. It is also the only place where the author tells the student to return to the material repeatedly across the semester (p. 13). A topic that is taught twice (algebraically in Week 3, graphically in Week 4), summarised twice, and drilled 43 times is the clearest possible signal.

**Why Differentiation is second, not third.** Although Session 4 (integration) is the single longest session, differentiation is spread across **two** sessions (S2 + S3 = 37 pp.) and receives **two separate Conclusion + Summary pairs** — more dedicated review scaffolding than any other topic in the book. It also has the widest spread of distinct examinable *procedures* (first principles, four rules with proofs, chain rule, implicit, higher‑order), each of which is an independent question type. The Chain Rule's 9‑page treatment (pp. 71–79) is the longest sub‑topic in the book, and the "‼‼" on p. 68 is the strongest single typographic emphasis the author uses anywhere.

**Why Integration I is third.** It has the widest file distribution (13 of 19 files) and the most elaborate chapter summary (six numbered points, pp. 124–126), plus the book's only purpose‑built comparison table. It ranks below differentiation only because a slice of its slide content (integration by parts, trigonometric substitution, partial fractions) exists **only in the Week 8 deck and recap and is entirely absent from the 130‑page textbook** — meaning the textbook‑examinable core of Integration I is narrower (pp. 86–105) than the differentiation core.

**Why Integration II is fourth.** It is unambiguously high‑yield — 20 applied exercises, seven of the ten figures, five supporting slide files — but it is *dependent* on Integration I (you cannot compute a volume without the FTC and substitution). Its rank reflects prerequisite ordering as much as weight: it is the highest‑value topic **per mark** on a paper, but the lowest‑value to attempt before topic 3 is secure.

**Why Functions is fifth.** It has enormous slide coverage (65 slides, the largest of any topic) and opens both the Session 1 Summary and the Full Course Summary, but it is the only topic in this list **without a dedicated textbook section, exercise set, or Conclusion of its own** — the 130‑page text folds it into Session 1's title and two summary lines on p. 47. Its exam value is therefore high as *enabling* knowledge (domain/range restrictions, function types, graph reading) that is reused inside limit, derivative and integral questions, rather than as standalone question stock.

### Borderline case you should be aware of (ranked 6th)

**Improper Integrals — convergence, divergence and the Comparison Test.** This topic narrowly missed the top five and deserves an explicit note rather than silent omission:

- **In favour:** 41 hits for `improper`, 19 for `comparison test`, 55 hits in the Week 9 recap alone. It has a fully dedicated week (`Week_9_Comparison_Test_for_Improper_Integrals_(PDF).pdf`, 17 slides) with three explicit learning objectives (slide 2), a TASK slide (14), a SUMMARY slide (15), a dedicated recap deck, and it is the **first topic listed in the Weeks 9–12 block of the Full Course Summary (p. 8)**, with its own case study ("Analysing Temperature Conduction in Materials") and the lead "Question to ponder" (p. 10).
- **Against:** it has the smallest total footprint of any weekly topic (~21 pp./slides), and — verified by a full‑text search — **the word "improper" appears zero times in the 130‑page core textbook**. It exists only in the slide/recap layer.
- **Practical advice:** if your examiner draws from the weekly slide decks, treat this as a top‑five topic and study slides 4–13 ("IMPROPER INTEGRALS", "TYPE I IMPROPER INTEGRALS" ×3, "TYPE II IMPROPER INTEGRALS" ×2, "COMPARISON TEST FOR IMPROPER INTEGRALS" ×4). If the exam is built from the textbook, it cannot be examined at all. It is cheap to learn — budget one short session.

## 3. Stated limitations (no inference beyond the evidence)

- **No past exam papers, mark schemes, marking weights, question banks, syllabus weighting documents, or a README exist in this repository.** The ranking above is therefore derived from *pedagogical emphasis* (coverage, repetition, summaries, objectives, exercises, author emphasis) — the criteria requested — and not from any statement in the repository about what will actually be examined. No such statement exists in any of the 19 files.
- **Slide numbers are not printed on any deck.** Verified programmatically. All deck citations use PDF page position (= slide position) *plus* the exact slide title text so the slide can be located unambiguously either way.
- **Textbook page numbers are printed and verified**: the footer "Page N of 130" matches the PDF page index for all pages 2–130.
- **Some slide content is unreadable as text.** Formulas and worked examples on many slides are embedded images (e.g. Week 5: 114 images/24 slides; Week 8: 91 images/24 slides; Week 11: 88 images/19 slides). Where a slide's body is an image, only its title has been cited and no claim is made about the specific worked example it contains.
- **Three integration techniques — integration by parts, trigonometric substitution, and partial fractions — appear only in the Week 8 deck and Week 8 recap.** A full‑text search returns **zero** occurrences of "by parts" or "partial fraction" in the 130‑page textbook. This gap is reported, not resolved.
- **Improper integrals, L'Hôpital's Rule, parametric differentiation, and partial differentiation likewise appear only in the slide decks** and have zero occurrences in the textbook. (L'Hôpital: Week 3 deck slides 12–13; parametric/partial differentiation: Week 5 deck slides 14 and 21.)
- **Conversely, exponential growth & decay, half‑life and doubling time (textbook §3.7–3.8, pp. 80–83) appear only in the textbook** and in no slide deck.
- Where the extracted text of the core textbook is garbled by mathematical notation (a known artefact of the PDF's font encoding), only passages that could be read unambiguously have been quoted.
